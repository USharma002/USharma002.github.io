Fiber-Based Rendering & Monte Carlo Methods

January 1, 2024  ·  20-minute read

Introduction to Rendering

The rendering equation describes the total amount of light emitted from a point along a particular viewing direction, given a function for incoming light and a BRDF. It forms the theoretical foundation for all physically-based rendering systems.

Understanding this equation deeply is essential before moving on to practical algorithms like path tracing or bidirectional methods.

Assumptions

We assume light travels in straight lines, there is no participating media, and surfaces are either opaque Lambertian or specular. These are simplifications that hold well for most solid surfaces.

Relaxing these assumptions leads to more complex formulations such as the radiative transfer equation for volumetric media.

Components

The main components are: the emitted radiance term, the BRDF, the cosine foreshortening term, and the incoming radiance integral over the hemisphere of directions.

The Rendering Equation

The classic form integrates over the hemisphere of directions above a surface point. It was introduced by Kajiya in 1986 and remains the cornerstone of modern rendering research.

The equation captures both direct illumination from light sources and indirect illumination from inter-reflections between surfaces.

Alternate Formulations of the Rendering Equation

There are several equivalent reformulations that are useful for different Monte Carlo estimators and rendering algorithms.

Monte Carlo Basics

Monte Carlo integration estimates an integral by averaging random samples of the integrand divided by their probability density. This approach is dimension-agnostic, making it ideal for the high-dimensional integrals in light transport.

The method converges at a rate of O(1/√N) regardless of the number of dimensions, which is a significant advantage over deterministic quadrature for high-dimensional problems.

The Monte Carlo Estimator

Given N samples drawn from pdf p(x), the estimator is (1/N) Σ f(xᵢ)/p(xᵢ). This is unbiased — its expected value equals the true integral for any choice of sampling distribution p.

Importance Sampling

Variance is reduced by choosing a pdf that is proportional to the integrand. When the sampling distribution matches the integrand exactly, variance drops to zero. In practice, we approximate this ideal distribution.

Cosine-weighted hemisphere sampling, for instance, matches the cosine foreshortening factor in the rendering equation and significantly reduces variance compared to uniform sampling.

Path Tracing

Path tracing is the canonical Monte Carlo rendering algorithm. It traces rays from the camera, bounces them through the scene, and accumulates light contributions along each path. It is unbiased and converges to the correct answer with enough samples.

Multiple Importance Sampling (MIS)

MIS combines estimates from multiple sampling strategies using balance or power heuristics. It is particularly effective when the integrand has both specular peaks and broad diffuse regions that no single strategy handles well.
