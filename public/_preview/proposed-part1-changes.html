**Changing variables between domains.** The same integral can be written over
directions or over surface points, and converting between the two is the single
most reused piece of machinery in the second half of these notes. If a direction
$\boldsymbol{\omega}$ from $\mathbf{x}$ hits a surface at $\mathbf{y}$, the solid
angle subtended by a patch $\mathrm{d}A(\mathbf{y})$ shrinks with distance and
with how obliquely the patch is turned away:

$$
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{d}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \frac{\lvert\cos\theta_y\rvert}{\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x} \rVert^{2}}\, \mathrm{d}A(\mathbf{y}),
\label{eq:solid-angle-to-area}
\end{equation}
$$

where $\theta_y$ is the angle between $-\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and the normal
$\mathbf{n}_y$ at $\mathbf{y}$. The inverse-square factor is the same one from
the assumptions; the cosine is the same foreshortening as in
$\eqref{eq:projected-area}$, now applied at the *receiving* end.

The ratio in $\eqref{eq:solid-angle-to-area}$ is a **Jacobian**: the local
stretch factor of the map $\boldsymbol{\omega} \mapsto \mathbf{y}$. Whenever a
density is carried from one domain to another it must be divided by the
Jacobian of the map, so that probability mass is preserved:

$$
\begin{equation}
p_A(\mathbf{y}) = p_\sigma(\boldsymbol{\omega}) \left\lvert \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}}{\partial \mathbf{y}} \right\rvert
= p_\sigma(\boldsymbol{\omega})\, \frac{\lvert\cos\theta_y\rvert}{\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x} \rVert^{2}}.
\label{eq:density-change-of-variables}
\end{equation}
$$

This is worth internalising in its general form, because every learned sampler
in the second half is a change of variables in disguise. A normalizing flow maps
uniform samples to a target distribution and pays for it with a Jacobian
determinant; a warp between the unit square and the sphere pays for it with the
Jacobian of that parameterization; a mixture defined in one coordinate frame and
evaluated in another pays for it with the Jacobian of the frame change. In every
case the rule is the same as $\eqref{eq:density-change-of-variables}$: **transform
the sample, then divide the density by how much the transform stretched space.**

#### Sampling by Inverting the CDF

The CDF is not just a way to describe a distribution — it is the standard way to
*draw from* one. Suppose $U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1)$ and we set

$$
\begin{equation}
X = F_X^{-1}(U).
\label{eq:inverse-cdf}
\end{equation}
$$

Then $X$ is distributed according to $F_X$. The proof is one line:

$$
P(X \le x) = P\!\left(F_X^{-1}(U) \le x\right) = P\!\left(U \le F_X(x)\right) = F_X(x),
$$

using that $F_X$ is non-decreasing and that $P(U \le u) = u$ for a uniform
variate.

**This is the mechanism behind every sampler in these notes.** Drawing a cosine
weighted direction, picking a point on an area light, choosing which of several
techniques to use — all of them are an inverse CDF applied to uniform random
numbers. Sampling a 2D distribution works the same way, one dimension at a time:
sample the marginal $p(u)$ by inverting its CDF, then sample the conditional
$p(v \mid u)$ by inverting *its* CDF. That two-step procedure is called
**hierarchical sample warping**, and it is exactly what PPG's directional
quadtree and the distribution-factorization method both do.

Seen this way, a sampler is a **map from the unit hypercube to the domain you
care about**. The uniform numbers are the raw material; the map is the design
choice. That reframing is what the second half of these notes is about: the
learned methods differ mainly in how expressive a map they can build and how
cheaply they can invert it and evaluate its Jacobian.

#### Mixture Models

A single Gaussian, or a single lobe of any shape, is rarely enough to describe a
real distribution. A **mixture model** builds an expressive density out of simple
parts:

$$
\begin{equation}
D(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i \, \frac{G_i(x; \theta_i)}{K_i},
\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i = 1,
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
$$

where each $G_i$ is a simple component with parameters $\theta_i$, each $K_i$ is
its normalization constant, and the weights $A_i$ form a discrete distribution
over components. Sampling is two-stage: pick a component $i$ with probability
$A_i$, then sample from that component. Evaluating the density means summing all
$N$ terms.

Two properties decide whether a given component shape is usable in a renderer:

*   **Normalisability.** $K_i = \int G_i$ must have a closed form. Without it the
    mixture cannot be turned into a valid PDF cheaply, and the estimator's $1/p$
    term is unavailable. This is a hard constraint rather than a convenience —
    it is the stated reason the NASG paper designs a new distribution rather than
    adopting an existing anisotropic one.
*   **Direct sampling.** There must be a way to draw from $G_i$ without
    rejection, i.e. an invertible CDF in the sense of
    $\eqref{eq:inverse-cdf}$.

Fitting a mixture to data is classically done with **expectation–maximization
(EM)**, which alternates between assigning responsibility for each sample to the
components and re-estimating each component from the samples it owns. EM is
cheap and well understood, but it only converges to a local optimum — a point
the SD-tree paper makes when it argues for a hierarchical structure instead.
The neural methods later replace EM with gradient descent on a divergence,
which is the subject of the next section.

### Learning a Distribution

Everything above treats $p$ as something we *pick* from a catalogue of analytic
options. The alternative is to *fit* it: choose a family of densities
$q(x; \theta)$ with adjustable parameters $\theta$, and tune $\theta$ so that
$q$ approaches the ideal $p^{*} \propto f$. Two questions follow — what to
measure, and how to move.

#### What to minimize

The natural target is a **divergence**, a scalar measuring how far $q$ is from
$p^{*}$. Two appear throughout the second half.

**Kullback–Leibler divergence.** The standard choice:

$$
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*} \,\|\, q_\theta) = \int_{\Omega} p^{*}(x) \log \frac{p^{*}(x)}{q(x;\theta)} \, \mathrm{d}x.
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
$$

It is zero exactly when $q = p^{*}$ and positive otherwise. It is not symmetric:
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p)$, and the order
matters in practice. The form above penalises $q$ being small where $p^{*}$ is
large — that is, it punishes *missing* bright directions, which is the failure
mode that actually hurts a renderer.

**Pearson $\chi^{2}$ divergence.** The other choice, and the more directly
motivated one here:

$$
\begin{equation}
D_{\chi^{2}}(p^{*} \,\|\, q_\theta) = \int_{\Omega} \frac{\left(p^{*}(x) - q(x;\theta)\right)^{2}}{q(x;\theta)} \, \mathrm{d}x.
\label{eq:chi2}
\end{equation}
$$

This one is worth dwelling on, because **minimizing it is the same thing as
minimizing the variance of the estimator**. Recall from
$\eqref{eq:mc-variance}$ that the variance is governed by
$\mathbb{E}[(f/p)^{2}]$; expanding $\eqref{eq:chi2}$ recovers exactly that
quantity up to constants. So the two framings — "make $q$ close to $f$" and
"make the estimator low-variance" — are not merely related, they are the same
optimisation written twice.

#### The normalization problem

There is an obstacle. We want $q \propto f$, but $p^{*} = f / F$ requires the
normalizing constant $F = \int f$, which is the integral we are trying to
compute in the first place. The same circularity as in
[The Catch](#the-catch).

The way out is that the gradient does not need $F$. Since $F$ is a constant with
respect to $\theta$, it scales every gradient uniformly and changes only the
effective step size, not the direction of descent. So the unnormalized $f$ can
be used directly in a stochastic gradient estimate. This works cleanly for a
*single* integral; when many conditional distributions are learned at once —
one per shading point, which is precisely the path-guiding setting — each has
its own $F$, and the argument no longer holds exactly. That caveat is a stated
limitation of the neural importance sampling work.

#### How to move

Since the divergence is an integral, its gradient is estimated from samples
rather than computed exactly, giving a **stochastic gradient**. Parameters are
updated by descending it:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \widehat{\nabla_\theta D},
$$

with $\eta$ the **learning rate**. In practice an adaptive optimizer such as
**Adam**, which keeps running estimates of gradient mean and variance to scale
each parameter's step individually, replaces plain descent. Two consequences
carry into the papers: stochastic gradients do not settle at an optimum but
oscillate around it unless $\eta$ is decayed, and a poorly chosen $\eta$ can let
a distribution collapse — both are failure modes reported later.

When $q$ is represented by a neural network, the parameters $\theta$ are the
network weights. The networks involved are small: a **multilayer perceptron
(MLP)** of a few fully connected layers, typically with **ReLU** activations,
and a **softmax** on the output whenever the network must emit something that
sums to one — bin probabilities, or mixture weights $A_i$ from
$\eqref{eq:mixture}$. Nothing deeper is needed, because the network is evaluated
inside the rendering loop, where its cost is charged against the
[efficiency](#measuring-error-and-efficiency) of every sample it guides.

WITH

The **power heuristic** often reduces variance even further. For an exponent $\beta$, the power heuristic is 

$$
w_i(x) = \frac{(n_ip_i(x))^\beta}{\sum_j (n_j p_j(x))^\beta}
$$

In practice $\beta = 2$ is the conventional choice; $\beta = 1$ recovers the
balance heuristic exactly.

**The single-sample form.** Instead of taking one sample from *every* technique,
we can pick one technique at random and take a single sample from it. If
technique $q$ is chosen with probability $c$ and technique $p_{f_s}$ with
probability $1-c$, the sample is effectively drawn from the **mixture density**

$$
\begin{equation}
q'(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = c\, q(\boldsymbol{\omega}) + (1-c)\, p_{f_s}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),
\label{eq:mis-mixture}
\end{equation}
$$

and dividing by $q'$ gives an unbiased estimator with no explicit weights at
all — the balance heuristic is recovered automatically. This form matters for
the second half: when a learned distribution is combined with BSDF sampling,
the thing that should be fitted to the target is $q'$, not $q$, because $q'$ is
what samples are actually drawn from. The selection probability $c$ can itself
be learned, and one of the methods later does exactly that.

It is worth stating how strong this is. If $p$ really were proportional to $f$,
then a **single sample** would suffice: the ratio $f(X)/p(X)$ is the same
constant $I$ no matter which $X$ you happen to draw, so one evaluation finds the
scaling factor and the answer exactly. Every extra sample in a real renderer is
paying for the gap between $p$ and $f$. That is the sense in which the whole
second half of these notes is one long attempt to close that gap.
